## Purpose

This chapter defines what would be observed about the governed services, how
signals relate to alerting and response, and how observability binds to evidence.
It defines observability obligations only; it deploys no telemetry and raises no
alert.

## Signals and their context

Observability signals are recorded in REG-1102 as observability-signal records.
Each signal names its context, its sensitivity, its owner, its alert dependency,
and its evidence binding. A signal without context is meaningless: latency,
volume, and error signals are interpreted only within an organization, season,
and jurisdiction context. Signal sensitivity is recorded because some signals
touch personal information or restricted evidence and must be handled accordingly.

## Telemetry, alerting, detection, response, recovery, and reconciliation

The observability chain is defined as a sequence of distinct concerns that must
not be collapsed:

- **Telemetry** is the raw observation of the service.
- **Alerting** is the governed raising of a signal breach to a responsible owner.
- **Detection** is recognising that a condition of concern exists.
- **Response** is the governed action taken in consequence.
- **Recovery** is the return of the service to an accepted operating state.
- **Reconciliation** is the subsequent governed confirmation that state is
  consistent after response and recovery.

Detection of a condition is distinct from response to it, and response is distinct
from recovery, which is in turn distinct from reconciliation. Each step depends on
the previous and none is assumed by the presence of another.

## Alert dependency

Every observability signal records its alert dependency: an alert on a signal is
meaningful only when a threshold, a responsible owner, and a governed response
procedure exist. A signal with no owner and no response procedure is not an alert;
it is unattended noise, and is recorded as such.

## Evidence binding

Every signal binds its observations to evidence: the environment, configuration,
version, identity, organization, jurisdiction, and time under which it was
observed. An observation that cannot be bound to its operating context cannot serve
as operational evidence, as defined further in Chapter V11-09.
